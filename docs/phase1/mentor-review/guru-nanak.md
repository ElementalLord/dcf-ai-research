## Life and context

- Guru Nanak is conventionally dated 1469–1539; most detailed narratives of his early life and travels survive in later Sikh textual traditions. [S1]
- Janamsakhis agree on broad biographical elements such as family and the general outline of Guru Nanak’s life, but their agreement does not turn every detail into independently verified history. [S2]
- Mann notes Puratan evidence of an early Sikh self-understanding as not simply following surrounding religious practice. [S3]
- Mann reports that the Puratan Janamsakhi narrates four long journeys after Kartarpur; this is evidence of the Puratan tradition’s itinerary, not a standalone verification of each journey. [S3]
- Kartarpur became the principal early Sikh center during Guru Nanak’s final decades, where he lived with his family and a community formed around him. [S1]

## Qualities and values

- In the Mūl Mantra, Ik Oankar expresses the affirmation of the One; English paraphrases are interpretive renderings of a compact Punjabi/Gurmukhi formula. [S4]
- Japji asks how falsehood is overcome and answers by walking in accord with hukam; the cited formulation is an attributed scriptural teaching rather than a neutral historical report. [S5]
- Japji’s opening attributes fearlessness and freedom from enmity to the One; these attributes anchor Sikh accounts of divine unity. [S6]
- Japji’s opening frames realization through the Guru’s grace, joining divine devotion with a disciplined spiritual path. [S6]

## Key contributions

- The concept of hukam appears at the beginning of Japji, the opening composition of the Adi Granth/Guru Granth Sahib. [S5]
- The historiography entry identifies Bhai Gurdas’s Vars as early Sikh verse of high importance for Guru Nanak’s mission and that of his successors, though comparatively sparse in biography. [S7]
- The Mūl Mantra is the opening formula of the Guru Granth Sahib and recurs in whole or part at major textual openings. [S4]
- SGPC describes the Guru Granth Sahib’s compilation as privileging verses praising God and criticizing superstition and caste. [S8]
- SGPC names Japji and Asa-di-Var as Guru Nanak compositions and Punjabi literary classics within the Guru Granth Sahib. [S8]
- The Sangat entry connects Guru Nanak’s travels with groups of disciples who gathered to recite his hymns. [S9]

## Challenges and limits

- SGPC presents caste as religiously irrelevant before God, while historical Sikh societies still had to negotiate caste in social practice. [S10]

## Leadership and relationships

- Bhai Gurdas’s remembered list of early disciples includes Mardana, identified as a Muslim mirasi or bard; this helps explain why the Guru’s musical companionship matters in Sikh memory. [S9]
- Kartarpur is associated with Bhai Lehna’s instruction by Guru Nanak and his emergence as Guru Angad, the next Guru. [S1]
- The Kartarpur entry records the succession of Guru Angad in a reference account; it is central to Sikh memory of a non-hereditary line of Gurus. [S1]
- Gurinder Singh Mann interprets the early Kartarpur home-community as large enough for congregational prayer, food preparation and service, and visitors. [S3]

## Contemporary relevance

- Sangat denotes fellowship in devotion and moral formation; it also became a social unit oriented to brotherhood, equality, and seva. [S9]
- The langar tradition has people sit in pangat and share food without distinction of caste or status, giving community practice a concrete egalitarian form. [S11]
