## Important qualities

- Hardy especially admired Ramanujan's insight into algebraic formulas and transformations of infinite series. [S4] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_
- Hardy later characterized the 1913 letter's author as a mathematician of exceptional originality and power. [S1] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_

## Main contributions

- As a student, Ramanujan studied G. S. Carr's compendium of mathematical results, an important resource for his independent mathematical learning. [S2] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_
- Hardy and Ramanujan developed an asymptotic formula for the partition function p(n). [S5] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_
- Near the end of his life, Ramanujan sent Hardy examples of his new mock theta functions. [S6] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_
- Ramanujan and Hardy jointly published work on coefficients of modular functions in the Royal Society's Proceedings in 1918. [S1] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_
- Ramanujan published unusually effective approximations to pi in his work on modular equations. [S5] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_
- Ramanujan studied integer partitions and identified striking congruence patterns in partition counts. [S5] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_

## Life and historical/social context

- Ramanujan published mathematical work in India before going to Cambridge, including his 1911 Bernoulli-numbers paper. [S1] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_
- Ramanujan was born in Erode in 1887 and grew up chiefly in Kumbakonam in South India. [S2] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_
- Srinivasa Ramanujan was an Indian mathematician whose main contributions were to analytic number theory, alongside work on continued fractions and infinite series. [S2] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_
- A Mathematical Gazette account places Ramanujan at the Madras Port Trust when he first wrote to G. H. Hardy. [S3] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_

## Challenges and limits

- Ramanujan became seriously ill in 1917 and spent much of his remaining time in England in sanatoria. [S6] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_
- Ramanujan lost his Government College scholarship after concentrating on mathematics and neglecting other subjects. [S2] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_
- Ramanujan supported himself as a clerk at the Madras Port Trust while continuing mathematical research. [S2] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_
- Without a university mathematics education or regular access to current research, Ramanujan continued developing ideas independently. [S2] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_

## Leadership and relational characteristics

- Hardy's encouraging reply led to a sustained exchange of letters with Ramanujan. [S6] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_
- Ramanujan entered Trinity College, Cambridge, in 1914 and developed his work with Hardy and Littlewood's guidance. [S5] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_
- Ramanujan sent Hardy a long list of mathematical discoveries in 1913, beginning the correspondence that changed his research opportunities. [S6] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_
- The University of Madras awarded Ramanujan a two-year scholarship in 1913, enabling him to pursue research. [S2] _(approved claim; corpus_evidence_review)_
