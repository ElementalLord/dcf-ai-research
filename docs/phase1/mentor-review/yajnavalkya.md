## Era and region

- **Era:** Late Vedic: the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad is dated c. 700–500 BCE (a date for the text, not the person)
  - The Brihadaranyaka belongs to the earliest group of Upanishads, which scholars generally date between about 700 and 500 BCE, before the rise of Buddhism and Jainism; this is an estimate for the text, not a date for Yajnavalkya as a person. [S8]
- **Region:** Videha in the eastern Gangetic plain, according to scholarly readings of the Brahmana
  - Macdonell (1900) judged it highly probable, from passages of the Shatapatha Brahmana, that Yajnavalkya was a native of Videha, and described him as a pupil of Aruni and the chief authority of the Brahmana outside books 6–10. [S11]
  - Witzel places Yajnavalkya in the east, in Videha, because he is absent from the more western Shandilya books (6–10) of the Brahmana; but since the Upanishad counts him among Kuru-Panchala Brahmins, Witzel infers he was an immigrant to an eastern region that was adopting Vedic culture in the last centuries before the Buddha. [S7] [S11]

## Life and context

- Eggeling observes that Yajnavalkya’s opinion is often cited as authoritative in books 1–5 of the Shatapatha Brahmana but that he is never mentioned in books 6–9, where Shandilya is the authority; with differences of style and geography, he takes this as evidence of separate authorship or redaction. [S1]
- Hock argues that the account of Yajnavalkya’s disputations in Brihadaranyaka 3, which Brereton analysed as a ring composition, belongs to a larger ring composition spanning Brihadaranyaka 2.1–4.5 that may be a later expansion; the Yajnavalkya chapters are thus the product of layered composition rather than a single record. [S2]
- Lindquist’s study, as its publisher describes it, follows a "literary life" rather than a biography: from Yajnavalkya’s earliest mentions in ritual texts, through a developing biography in the Upanishads, to his role as an ancient sage in narrative literature, where he was seen as ritual authority, mystic and propagator of religious law. [S3]

## Qualities and values

- Brihadaranyaka 4.4.22 links knowledge of the self with renunciation: Brahmins seek the self through Vedic recitation, sacrifice, gifts, austerity and fasting; knowing it one becomes a sage, and those who desire it alone give up the wish for sons, wealth and worlds and wander as mendicants. [S4]
- In Brihadaranyaka 3.2.13 Yajnavalkya takes his questioner Artabhaga by the hand and withdraws to discuss a question in private rather than in public; the text reports that what they spoke of was karma: one becomes good by good action and bad by bad action. [S4]
- In Brihadaranyaka 3.7 Yajnavalkya teaches the self as the "inner controller" (antaryamin): the unseen seer, unheard hearer and unthought thinker, other than whom there is no seer, hearer or knower. [S4]
- In Brihadaranyaka 4.2.4 Yajnavalkya tells Janaka that the self "is not this, it is not that" (neti, neti): it cannot be grasped, destroyed, attached or bound. He declares that Janaka has reached fearlessness, and the king offers him the Videhas and himself in service. [S4]

## Key contributions

- "Neti neti" first occurs as a named teaching (ādeśa), "athāta ādeśo neti neti", at Shatapatha Brahmana 14.5.3.11 (Brihadaranyaka 2.3.6), in the passage that immediately precedes the Maitreyi dialogue, before Yajnavalkya begins to speak. [S5]
- The Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad’s closing lineage says that white sacrificial formulas from Āditya are declared by Yājñavalkya of the Vājasaneyi school; this is the text’s own account of the tradition. [S4]
- The Sanskrit of the formula is "sa eṣa neti nety ātmā" ("this self is not thus, not thus"), followed by a string of negations: ungraspable, undecaying, unattached, unbound. In the Madhyandina recension it stands at Shatapatha Brahmana 14.6.11.6, because the Upanishad is transmitted as the closing part of that Brahmana. [S5]
- Eggeling explains the White Yajurveda as a reform of older Yajus tradition: its school separated the sacrificial formulas from the explanatory prose, producing a distinct Samhita and Brahmana, and tradition ascribes the founding of this school, the Vajasaneyins, to Yajnavalkya Vajasaneya. [S1]

## Challenges and limits

- Shatapatha Brahmana 11.6.3 holds a short version of the contest at Janaka’s sacrifice: Janaka sets apart a thousand cows for the most learned Brahmin, Yajnavalkya has them driven off, and when challenged says he honours the most learned but simply wants the cows. [S6]
- Witzel stresses that nearly all early information about Yajnavalkya comes from two texts, the Shatapatha Brahmana and the slightly later Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, each transmitted in two recensions (Kanva and Madhyandina) that can be checked against one another. [S7]

## Leadership and relationships

- Brihadaranyaka 4.1 opens with Janaka asking Yajnavalkya whether he has come for cattle or for subtle disputation; Yajnavalkya answers that he has come for both. [S4]
- Brihadaranyaka 4.5.1 says Yajnavalkya had two wives: Maitreyi, called a brahmavadini (one who discourses on sacred knowledge), and Katyayani, said to have only "a woman’s knowledge". About to take up another mode of life, he proposes to settle his property between them. [S4]
- Brihadaranyaka 6.3.7 names Uddalaka Aruni as the teacher of his pupil "Vajasaneya Yajnavalkya", in the handing down of a ritual saying. [S4]
- Gargi returns in Brihadaranyaka 3.8 and tells the assembled Brahmins she will put two questions to Yajnavalkya, likening them to a warrior’s two arrows; if he answers them, she says, none of them will defeat him in debate about brahman. [S4]

## Contemporary relevance

- The Upanishads that preserve Yajnavalkya’s teaching were most influentially interpreted by Shankara (about 700 CE), the main proponent of non-dualist Advaita Vedanta, whose readings shaped Indian philosophy for centuries and dominated interpretation of the texts into the early twentieth century. [S8]
- Yajnavalkya’s positions were still cited in later philosophy outside Vedanta: Black notes that the Nyaya philosopher Gangesha (about 1300 CE) appears to quote the Upanishads for the claim that karmic retribution does not bind those who know the self, a position stated by Yajnavalkya. [S8]

### Vocation / Role within the Rishi Tradition

**Primary function: drashta, guru, darshana-founder, smriti-author, ashram-head** (2 approved claims)

- In the Shatapatha Brahmana Yajnavalkya appears as a ritual authority whose rulings are quoted: at 1.1.1.9, on whether a sacrificer should fast before the rite, he dissents from the rule of total fasting and says one should eat what "counts as not eaten". [S6]
- In the Brahmana, Witzel notes, Yajnavalkya appears above all as a Yajurveda ritualist discussing minute details of the solemn (Shrauta) ritual in about a dozen passages; his opinions are sometimes clever or innovative, but not always followed even by his own school. [S7]

### Tapas and Sadhana

**Specific spiritual practice or discipline associated with the Rishi** (3 approved claims)

- Brihadaranyaka 4.4.22 links knowledge of the self with renunciation: Brahmins seek the self through Vedic recitation, sacrifice, gifts, austerity and fasting; knowing it one becomes a sage, and those who desire it alone give up the wish for sons, wealth and worlds and wander as mendicants. [S4]
- The Maitreyi dialogue ends with the question "whereby would one understand the understander?"; Yajnavalkya tells her this instruction is immortality, and the text then says he departed. [S4]
- Introducing the Maitreyi dialogue, Shankara says its purpose is to enjoin renunciation of the world, the giving up of all means, as a subsidiary step: a doctrinal use of the episode by later Advaita Vedanta. [S9]

### Philosophical / Doctrinal Position

**Core teaching or metaphysical insight distinctively associated with the Rishi** (12 approved claims)

- In Brihadaranyaka 3.2.13 Yajnavalkya takes his questioner Artabhaga by the hand and withdraws to discuss a question in private rather than in public; the text reports that what they spoke of was karma: one becomes good by good action and bad by bad action. [S4]
- In Brihadaranyaka 3.7 Yajnavalkya teaches the self as the "inner controller" (antaryamin): the unseen seer, unheard hearer and unthought thinker, other than whom there is no seer, hearer or knower. [S4]
- In Brihadaranyaka 4.2.4 Yajnavalkya tells Janaka that the self "is not this, it is not that" (neti, neti): it cannot be grasped, destroyed, attached or bound. He declares that Janaka has reached fearlessness, and the king offers him the Videhas and himself in service. [S4]
- In Brihadaranyaka 4.4.5 Yajnavalkya states the link between action and destiny: as one acts and conducts oneself, so one becomes; and since desire shapes resolve and resolve shapes action, a person obtains what his action (karma) earns. [S4]

**Relationship to major currents such as Vedanta, Samkhya, Mimamsa, etc.** (2 approved claims)

- The Upanishads do not teach one doctrine: Black notes that Yajnavalkya equates atman with brahman (Brihadaranyaka 4.4.5), whereas Uddalaka Aruni, source of the phrase "tat tvam asi", never uses the word brahman. [S8]
- Yajnavalkya’s views were contested within the Upanishads themselves: Black points out that Prajapati in the Chandogya Upanishad treats as false or incomplete the account of the self as dreamless sleep, which Yajnavalkya calls the highest goal and highest bliss when teaching Janaka. [S8]

**Unique or ahead-of-their-time conceptual contribution** (3 approved claims)

- Black reads Yajnavalkya’s statements on karma as an early, undeveloped theory: present actions shape the future, but he presents this more as a reason to act well than as fatalism, and does not explain the mechanism of rebirth. [S8]
- On linguistic grounds Witzel argues that the Janaka dialogue contains new coinages: "advaita" (non-duality) occurs in Vedic literature only at Brihadaranyaka 4.3.32 and its Brahmana parallel, and he regards it as a word coined by Yajnavalkya. [S7]
- The publisher’s description of Lindquist’s monograph calls Yajnavalkya the most important ancient Indian literary figure before the Buddha and lists the "firsts" associated with him in the literature: the first thorough discussion of brahman and atman, the first theory of karma and rebirth, the first to renounce household life, and the first to debate women on religious questions. [S3]

### Inner Qualities (Rishi-specific virtues)

**Viveka, samatva, akrodha, satya-nishtha and other evidenced virtues** (1 approved claim)

- Witzel characterizes the sayings attributed to Yajnavalkya as witty, sarcastic and sometimes derisive of the ritual, of others and of himself, and remarks that self-deprecating humour is unusual for a Vedic personality. [S7]

**Universality of vision vs. sectarian narrowness** (2 approved claims)

- When the Brahmins want to challenge King Janaka to a debate, Yajnavalkya dissuades them on grounds of social rank: defeating a man of the ruling class would win them nothing, while losing to him would shame Brahmins. He then follows the king alone. [S6]
- Black notes a social limit in the teaching: Yajnavalkya assumes that those who know the self will mainly be Brahmin men, even while saying that knowledge of the self can make one a Brahmin. [S8]

### Relational / Social Role

**Advisory relationships with kings or dynasties** (5 approved claims)

- Brihadaranyaka 4.1 opens with Janaka asking Yajnavalkya whether he has come for cattle or for subtle disputation; Yajnavalkya answers that he has come for both. [S4]
- In Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 3.1, King Janaka of Videha holds a sacrifice attended by Brahmins of the Kuru-Panchala country and stakes a thousand cows, with gold tied to their horns, to find out which of them is the most learned; this is the setting of Yajnavalkya’s great debate. [S4]
- In Shatapatha Brahmana 11.3.1, Janaka of Videha tests Yajnavalkya on the daily fire offering (Agnihotra) by removing each possible offering in turn; Yajnavalkya answers that if nothing at all were left, truth would still be offered in faith, and the king gives him a hundred cows. [S6]
- In Shatapatha Brahmana 11.6.2, Janaka questions travelling Brahmins about the Agnihotra; he judges that Yajnavalkya has inquired into it most closely and gives him a hundred cows, but says that not even he knows the full course of the two libations, and drives away. [S6]

**Role in Ramayana / Mahabharata narratives when applicable** (1 approved claim)

- In a chapter of the Mahabharata’s Shanti Parva spoken in Yajnavalkya’s voice, the Sun ("the Maker of light") promises him that the Vedas and Upanishads will appear in him by inward light and that he will edit the entire Shatapatha: an epic account of how he received his Veda. [S12]

**Interactions with other rishis: collaboration, rivalry, debate** (8 approved claims)

- Brihadaranyaka 4.5.1 says Yajnavalkya had two wives: Maitreyi, called a brahmavadini (one who discourses on sacred knowledge), and Katyayani, said to have only "a woman’s knowledge". About to take up another mode of life, he proposes to settle his property between them. [S4]

- In Brihadaranyaka 3.6, Gargi Vachaknavi asks Yajnavalkya what each level of the world is "woven" upon; when she asks what the worlds of Brahma are woven on, he warns her not to question too much lest her head fall off, and she falls silent. [S4]
- In the earlier telling at Brihadaranyaka 2.4, Maitreyi asks whether the whole earth filled with wealth would make her immortal; Yajnavalkya says wealth gives no hope of immortality, and she asks instead to be taught what he knows. [S4]

### Historical and Textual Context

**Approximate era / yuga placement and caveats around dating** (1 approved claim)

- The Brihadaranyaka belongs to the earliest group of Upanishads, which scholars generally date between about 700 and 500 BCE, before the rise of Buddhism and Jainism; this is an estimate for the text, not a date for Yajnavalkya as a person. [S8]

**Textual layers where the Rishi appears** (6 approved claims)

- Shatapatha Brahmana 11.6.3 holds a short version of the contest at Janaka’s sacrifice: Janaka sets apart a thousand cows for the most learned Brahmin, Yajnavalkya has them driven off, and when challenged says he honours the most learned but simply wants the cows. [S6]
- Eggeling observes that Yajnavalkya’s opinion is often cited as authoritative in books 1–5 of the Shatapatha Brahmana but that he is never mentioned in books 6–9, where Shandilya is the authority; with differences of style and geography, he takes this as evidence of separate authorship or redaction. [S1]
- Hock argues that the account of Yajnavalkya’s disputations in Brihadaranyaka 3, which Brereton analysed as a ring composition, belongs to a larger ring composition spanning Brihadaranyaka 2.1–4.5 that may be a later expansion; the Yajnavalkya chapters are thus the product of layered composition rather than a single record. [S2]
- Macdonell argued from the lineage lists that the first two parts of the Brihadaranyaka, the Madhu section and the Yajnavalkya section, were independent Upanishads for nine generations within the White Yajurveda school before a teacher joined them, with a third part of supplementary matter added later. [S11]

**Distinguish possibly multiple figures sharing the same name** (3 approved claims)

[S3]
- The law book known as the Yajnavalkya Smriti or Dharmashastra is dated by its Harvard edition to the fourth or fifth century CE and described as composed by an anonymous author under the Guptas and ascribed to the Upanishadic philosopher, who serves as its narrator; it is a later attributed work, not evidence for the Vedic figure’s life. [S13]
- Wujastyk shows that Yajnavalkya’s name was attached to at least two quite different yoga texts that circulated in medieval India, and suggests the association was inspired by a remark in the Yajnavalkya Smriti (fourth to fifth century CE) about a yoga teaching "taught by me". [S14]
