## Important qualities

- Brihadaranyaka 4.4.22 links knowledge of the self with renunciation: Brahmins seek the self through Vedic recitation, sacrifice, gifts, austerity and fasting; knowing it one becomes a sage, and those who desire it alone give up the wish for sons, wealth and worlds and wander as mendicants. [S5] _(draft; awaiting human decision; ai_candidate)_
- In Brihadaranyaka 3.2.13 Yajnavalkya takes his questioner Artabhaga by the hand and withdraws to discuss a question in private rather than in public; the text reports that what they spoke of was karma: one becomes good by good action and bad by bad action. [S5] _(draft; awaiting human decision; ai_candidate)_
- In Brihadaranyaka 3.7 Yajnavalkya teaches the self as the "inner controller" (antaryamin): the unseen seer, unheard hearer and unthought thinker, other than whom there is no seer, hearer or knower. [S5] _(draft; awaiting human decision; ai_candidate)_
- In Brihadaranyaka 4.2.4 Yajnavalkya tells Janaka that the self "is not this, it is not that" (neti, neti): it cannot be grasped, destroyed, attached or bound. He declares that Janaka has reached fearlessness, and the king offers him the Videhas and himself in service. [S5] _(draft; awaiting human decision; ai_candidate)_

## Main contributions

- "Neti neti" first occurs as a named teaching (ādeśa), "athāta ādeśo neti neti", at Shatapatha Brahmana 14.5.3.11 (Brihadaranyaka 2.3.6), in the passage that immediately precedes the Maitreyi dialogue, before Yajnavalkya begins to speak. [S6] _(draft; awaiting human decision; ai_candidate)_
- The lineage at the end of the Brihadaranyaka states that "these white sacrificial formulas which come from Aditya" (the Sun) are declared by Yajnavalkya of the Vajasaneyi school: the Upanishad’s own link between him and the White (Shukla) Yajurveda. [S5] _(draft; awaiting human decision; ai_candidate)_
- The Sanskrit of the formula is "sa eṣa neti nety ātmā" ("this self is not thus, not thus"), followed by a string of negations: ungraspable, undecaying, unattached, unbound. In the Madhyandina recension it stands at Shatapatha Brahmana 14.6.11.6, because the Upanishad is transmitted as the closing part of that Brahmana. [S6] _(draft; awaiting human decision; ai_candidate)_
- Eggeling explains the White Yajurveda as a reform of older Yajus tradition: its school separated the sacrificial formulas from the explanatory prose, producing a distinct Samhita and Brahmana, and tradition ascribes the founding of this school, the Vajasaneyins, to Yajnavalkya Vajasaneya. [S2] _(draft; awaiting human decision; ai_candidate)_
- The Upanishads that preserve Yajnavalkya’s teaching were most influentially interpreted by Shankara (about 700 CE), the main proponent of non-dualist Advaita Vedanta, whose readings shaped Indian philosophy for centuries and dominated interpretation of the texts into the early twentieth century. [S7] _(draft; awaiting human decision; ai_candidate)_

- Hock argues that the account of Yajnavalkya’s disputations in Brihadaranyaka 3, which Brereton analysed as a ring composition, belongs to a larger ring composition spanning Brihadaranyaka 2.1–4.5 that may be a later expansion; the Yajnavalkya chapters are thus the product of layered composition rather than a single record. [S3] _(draft; awaiting human decision; ai_candidate)_
- Lindquist’s study, as its publisher describes it, follows a "literary life" rather than a biography: from Yajnavalkya’s earliest mentions in ritual texts, through a developing biography in the Upanishads, to his role as an ancient sage in narrative literature, where he was seen as ritual authority, mystic and propagator of religious law. - When the Brahmins want to challenge King Janaka to a debate, Yajnavalkya dissuades them on grounds of social rank: defeating a man of the ruling class would win them nothing, while losing to him would shame Brahmins. He then follows the king alone. [S1] _(draft; awaiting human decision; ai_candidate)_

## Leadership and relational characteristics

- Brihadaranyaka 4.1 opens with Janaka asking Yajnavalkya whether he has come for cattle or for subtle disputation; Yajnavalkya answers that he has come for both. [S5] _(draft; awaiting human decision; ai_candidate)_
- Brihadaranyaka 4.5.1 says Yajnavalkya had two wives: Maitreyi, called a brahmavadini (one who discourses on sacred knowledge), and Katyayani, said to have only "a woman’s knowledge". About to take up another mode of life, he proposes to settle his property between them. [S5] _(draft; awaiting human decision; ai_candidate)_
- Brihadaranyaka 6.3.7 names Uddalaka Aruni as the teacher of his pupil "Vajasaneya Yajnavalkya", in the handing down of a ritual saying. [S5] _(draft; awaiting human decision; ai_candidate)_
- Gargi returns in Brihadaranyaka 3.8 and tells the assembled Brahmins she will put two questions to Yajnavalkya, likening them to a warrior’s two arrows; if he answers them, she says, none of them will defeat him in debate about brahman. [S5] _(draft; awaiting human decision; ai_candidate)_

## Withheld contested or sensitive material

- Eggeling notes that the story of Yajnavalkya disgorging the Yajus he had learned from Vaishampayana seems to appear first in the Puranas, and that Panini and Patanjali know the Taittiriya texts only as "promulgated by Tittiri"; the tale is therefore later tradition about the two Yajurveda schools, not early testimony. — **withheld:** contested_requires_two_independent_sources; 1 independent credible source(s).
- The Purana continues that Yajnavalkya praised the Sun, who appeared as a horse and gave him Yajus texts unknown to his former teacher; the Purana derives the school name Vajin from this, and says fifteen branches arose from Kanva and other pupils. — **withheld:** contested_requires_two_independent_sources; 1 independent credible source(s).
- Scholars disagree on whether the ritualist of the Brahmana and the philosopher of the Upanishad are the same Yajnavalkya: Horsch and Ruben doubted it, while Witzel, following Tsuji, treats him as one person but sees serious editorial changes and additions to his image in the Upanishad. — **withheld:** contested_requires_two_independent_sources; 1 independent credible source(s).
- In the Vishnu Purana’s story, Yajnavalkya refuses to join a penance for his teacher Vaishampayana, is ordered to give back what he has learned, and brings up the Yajus texts; the other pupils, as partridges (tittiri), pick them up, which the Purana offers as the origin of the Taittiriya (Black) Yajurveda. — **withheld:** contested_requires_two_independent_sources; 1 independent credible source(s).
- The debate closes in Brihadaranyaka 3.9.26 when Yajnavalkya demands that Shakalya identify "the Person taught in the Upanishads" on pain of his head falling off; Shakalya cannot, and the text says his head fell off and robbers carried away his bones. This is the narrative’s own claim, not a historical report. — **withheld:** contested_requires_two_independent_sources; 1 independent credible source(s).
- Witzel concludes that a nucleus of sayings and reports goes back to one person who was at once priest, teacher, philosopher and mystic, but that at the time the texts were redacted (he suggests about 150 BCE, with a question mark) hagiographic touches were added, such as Janaka giving all Videha to Yajnavalkya. — **withheld:** contested_requires_two_independent_sources; 1 independent credible source(s).
